Retuning: UET VII 74

The Old Babylonian “tuning text” U.7/80 (UET VII 74, with the duplicate UET VI/3 899) takes the sammûm through seven tunings. In each, six of the seven primary pairs are “clear” (zakû) and one is “not clear” (la zakû). Adjust one string and that pair clears, another goes unclear, and the tuning takes a new name. The diagram can be drawn two ways: as the seven-pointed star of CBS 1766, which lays out the same cycle with the strings in their own order, or as a modern ring of fifths.

Fourteen string pairs: CBS 10996

A Nippur(?) list of mathematical coefficients, CBS 10996, includes a short section naming fourteen pairs of strings (SA) by number. Babylonian theory had no word for an abstract fifth or fourth, only for these particular pairs. Seven strings give 21 pairs: the 14 named ones are exactly the pairs that are not neighbours.

How the list is written

The order of the numbers

The primaries are written 1·5, 2·6, 3·7, then 4·1, 5·2, 6·3, 7·4. Each secondary shares the primary’s second string: the first three end on it (7·5, 1·6, 2·7), the last four start from it (1·3, 2·4, 3·5, 4·6), where 3·1, 4·2, 5·3, 6·4 would continue the pattern. The tablet gives the list twice, as bare numbers and terms (ll. a–10) and again naming every string (ll. 11–24), in the same order both times (Kilmer 1971: 134–35).

Kilmer (1960: 299) saw the first strings advance line by line, “but to what purpose is not clear”. Duchesne-Guillemin (1963) took the turn from 7 back to 1 for a sign of a cyclic, heptatonic system. Kümmel (1970) showed that the primaries, followed in their written direction from the namesake pair, give the tuning chain. Wulstan (1971: 375–76) read 1·6 and 2·7 as 8→6 and 9→7, so that every secondary runs against its primary, and made this his rule for h.6. West (1994, n. 8) saw no principle and thought the order could not have mattered in practice. For Hagel (2005: 294–97) the primaries turn into fourths only to stay within seven strings, and the break in the secondaries is “most probably meaningless”: the compiler did not adjust them when the primaries turned. Bayer (2014: 66–70) reads each entry as a conjunction and the primaries as a “+4 or −3” mnemonic in which 1 stands for 8.

Counted round the seven strings, the primaries are uniformly +4; only the secondaries change, from +5 to +2. In string numbers the Wulstan and Dumbrill run rules on this page are the same (primaries up the numbers through a fifth, secondaries down them through a third, so 1·3 runs 3–2–1); they sound opposite only because Wulstan puts string 1 at the bottom and Dumbrill at the top.

Nine strings, two numberings

Nabnītu XXXII (U.3011 = UET VII 126) names nine strings palindromically, from the front string to the fifth and then from the “fourth behind” to the “behind”, and ends “9 strings”. CBS 10996 uses these names for strings 1–7, calling 6 the “fourth behind” and 7 the “third behind” (Kilmer 1960: 300 nn. 1, 3; 1971: 135). The names only count right on nine strings, so the list’s instrument had nine though the list stops at seven, as U.7/80 implies when it moves “1 and 8” and “2 and 9” together.

YBC 11381 (Payne 2010), Neo-Babylonian and probably from Uruk, numbers nine strings straight through, SA 1 to 9, each with a god and a benediction. Two notes use the old names. Above SA 3 a gloss adds SIG, “thin”, which completes the Nabnītu name sa.3.sa.sig, šalšu qatnu, “third, thin” (the other two glosses on that line give the reading of two words of the benediction; Payne 2010: 294). On the left edge, level with string 3, stands šal-šú uḫ-ri, “third behind”, that is string 7. Payne reads the edge note with string 3 as the interval 3·7, embūbu, so perhaps the tuning in which the songs were sung (2010: 297). CBS 10996 l. 15 names embūbu by these same two names, “third thin” and “third behind” (Kilmer 1971: 135), and in U.7/80 ll. 1–3 the string tightened to make embūbum clear is the “third thin”. That both names match is this page’s observation, not Payne’s argument. Its parallel BM 65217+66616 (Kilmer 1984) is a set of benedictions “of the singer for the king”; Kilmer suggests that the string marks the note on which a piece ends (or begins).

Dumbrill (n.d.) reads the nine SA as nine “enneatonic” scales from an extended U.7/80 cycle without octave identity; the tablet itself gives only string numbers and incipits, and his nine scales are the seven tunings plus two repeated an apotome higher (see Retuning). Gibson (2021: 95–96) takes the tablets to show that both numberings were known, and notes that Enmešarra stands on string 2 in BM 65217+66616 but on string 9 here: perhaps 2 ≡ 9.

Around the tablet: music at Ugarit

What the Ugaritic texts say about song, singers, the lyre and Nikkal, after Koitabashi 1985–1998.

A genre named by its tuning

The colophon calls h.6 “a song of the nīd qabli”. Koitabashi (1996: 23–25) sets it beside Mesopotamian hymn colophons, which give the genre and then the copyist: TU 57 ends “balag of Enlil, not finished, for its singing”, written by Nidintu-Anu, lamentation priest (kalû) of Anu. The Middle Assyrian catalogue KAR 158 has a heading irâtu ša nīd qabli, “irtu-songs of the nīd qabli” (viii 49). Where adab, balag or tigi name an instrument and the Psalms’ “song of ascents” an occasion, this genre is named by its tuning. He hears it as Lydian, with Kilmer, Crocker and Brown, where Vitale heard Dorian: read up from string 1, nīd qabli has the Lydian’s steps, read down, the Dorian’s.

Instructions for performers

Koitabashi compares the other ways the ancient Near East told performers what to do. The Late Babylonian Converse Tablet puts drum names (meze, šem) in its left margin, vowel signs inside the text, and insertions ending ana SIR-ru, “to be sung”, in the right margin. Psalm headings name the leader, the accompaniment (“with strings”) and a tune (“to the Doe of the Dawn”, “Do not destroy”). Egyptian reliefs put hand-signallers beside the players: for Hickmann, thumb to forefinger gives the base note, the open hand the fifth above. h.6 alone is a letter notation that sets out a part in detail; Mesopotamian in its terms and script, it was probably brought to Ugarit by Hurrians (1996: 19–23; 1998: 379).

Singers

Ugaritic administrative lists name šrm, “singers” (from šr, “to sing”): a “singer of Ugarit”, a “singer of Ashtart”, a songstress (šrt). They drew rations as “personnel of the king” (bnš mlk), paid tribute (20 shekels in KTU 4.610), supplied bowmen, and some held land; Ymd is “the singer at the disposal of Ansny”, perhaps his teacher. In a monthly rite the singer sings ten times before the king, who then opens his hand (KTU 1.106). One singer, bn kdgdl, may bear a Hurrian name: Krispijn linked kdg to Kušuḫ, the Hurrian moon god (Koitabashi 1985; 1998: 365–68).

The lyre, a god

Ugaritic knr, the lyre (Akkadian kinnāru, known at Ebla, Mari, Alalakh and Emar; Hittite kinirtalla-, Greek kinyra), stands among the gods in the pantheon lists (KTU 1.47:32, 1.118:31; Akkadian dkinnāru, RS 20.24:31) and receives offerings (KTU 1.148:9). Koitabashi (1992) explains this by the lyre’s power over the feelings of gods and spirits, as when David’s lyre drives the evil spirit from Saul and Gudea’s temple harp is called a “counsellor”. In KTU 1.101 Anat “takes her lyre in her hand, puts the ‘cow’ (rimt) to her breast”: he takes rimt for the sound box, after Sumerian ab₂-za₃-mi₂, “cow of the sammûm”, the instrument of the tuning texts (1998: 373–74).

Nikkal in Ugaritic song

Nikkal is sung in Ugaritic too. KTU 1.24 opens “I sing of Nikkal-and-Ib” (ašr nkl w ib), tells of her marriage to the moon god Yariḫ, and then calls on the Kotharat, goddesses of marriage and birth. Koitabashi (1998: 386–87) reads it as a song for a royal wedding that asks for a safe childbirth, close to the fertility prayer that Thiel and Krispijn find in h.6.

Seven clauses

Hurrian marks the head of a clause with the enclitic -l(la), “they”, on its first word: tašal, išalla, wešal, uklal, niḫurašal, attayaštal. Laroche (1955) and Duchesne-Guillemin (1984) divide the text this way. Click to light a clause up on the copy.

    Sources and method

    What the page computes, what it approximates, and where each piece comes from.

    What is exact

    String pairs and their order follow CBS 10996. The seven tunings are generated from the UET VII 74 cycle in Pythagorean intonation (tone 9:8, limma 256:243; each adjustment moves one string by an apotome, 2187:2048). Interval names, cents and the clear/unclear status are computed, not looked up. Strings 8 and 9 follow 1 and 2 at the octave, as the double instructions “1 and 8”, “2 and 9” in the tuning text imply. The Hagel preset swaps in the just tuning of his MIDI files, the variant of Hagel 2005, Table 3 with c a pure fifth above f (pure embūbum); c pure to g (pure kitmum) or tempered between is under Fine tuning.

    What is entered, what is generated

    Five readings are played note for note from the published staff transcriptions: Wulstan 1971, Kilmer 1974 (Figs 7–8, including her re-ordering of the lines), Vitale 1982 (Tableau XIV), Dumbrill 2005 (Plate 5) and Dumbrill 2014. The others are generated from the scholar’s rules (edition, string order, how a term sounds, what the numeral counts); Duchesne-Guillemin’s rhythm is simplified, and Krispijn’s rests and Dumbrill’s maqām inflections are not reproduced. The Hagel rules were checked against his MIDI files: they reproduce his h.6 dyad for dyad and, on Laroche’s text, eleven of his sixteen fragment files exactly; his files stay on his site. Changing any setting switches a published reading to its rule-based approximation. Pitch frames are given where Gibson 2026 states them; otherwise they are the engine’s default and can be transposed.

    Readings of the signs

    Notation and lyrics are aligned after Casey Goranson’s comparison of Laroche, Kilmer, Duchesne-Guillemin, Dietrich & Loretz, Thiel and Krispijn; the Kilmer and Duchesne-Guillemin columns were then checked against Kilmer 1974 (Fig. 4) and Duchesne-Guillemin 1984, and Dumbrill’s column is his own 2005 reconstruction. Gibson 2026 is entered as D&L with his photographic queries: the 4s in ll. 8–10 may be 5s, l. 7 irbute is 1[+x], and the last numeral is damaged. Cuneiform is set in Noto Sans Cuneiform, a standard sign repertoire rather than the Ugarit ductus.
